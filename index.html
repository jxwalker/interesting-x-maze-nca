Synchronous: all tiles update at once, so the wave moves exactly one tile per tick. Off (asynchronous: cells wake up at random moments), it's slower and ragged, yet the numbers come out the same.

Each tile computes d ← 1 + min over open neighbours (Bellman–Ford relaxation, run as a cellular automaton). From all-"don't know" with everyone awake it is exact after max-distance ticks. A number ≥ n² is impossible, so it means "don't know": that's how cut-off tiles, which first count up, finally give up (the "count to infinity" problem of early internet routing).

A maze that solves itself

What you're looking at: Every tile is a tiny machine that can only see the four tiles touching it. Each one keeps a single number: how many steps it thinks it is from the brass pin. Tiles rise to the height of their number.

Why it matters: A new Google paper asks whether real reasoning can come from cells that only talk to their neighbours, with no one in charge. They train a small neural network to be the rule every cell follows (a neural cellular automaton) and it learns to solve mazes and Sudoku, survives damage and works on bigger boards. Here the rule is written by hand so you can watch it: my number is one more than my smallest neighbour's. A wave floods out, the maze becomes a staircase, and the way out is simply 'keep stepping downhill'.

How to use it

  1. Press Play: watch the numbers spread from the pin and the tiles climb into a staircase.
  2. When the wave reaches the flag, a lime trail walks downhill to the pin and the marble rolls home.
  3. Drag Cells awake per tick: fewer awake cells, slower and more ragged wave, same answer.
  4. Press Smash a patch, then watch the tiles fix themselves. Undo smash puts them back.
  5. Tap a wall to move it and watch the staircase reshape. Reset to example puts everything back.
